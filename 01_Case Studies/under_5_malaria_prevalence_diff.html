Urban–Rural Differences in Malaria Prevalence Among Children Under Five in Nigeria: Evidence from the 2023 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS)

Author

Taiwo Adegbola

Published

July 22, 2026

1. Background

Malaria remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality among children under five in Nigeria, which carries the highest global malaria burden of any single country. Transmission intensity and risk factors for malaria infection differ substantially by residence type — rural areas typically have greater proximity to mosquito breeding sites, lower net (ITN) coverage, reduced access to health facilities, and different housing construction (less screening, more open eaves), while urban areas generally benefit from better infrastructure, drainage, and healthcare access, though rapid unplanned urbanization can create localized high-risk pockets.

Understanding whether malaria prevalence differs meaningfully between urban and rural children is central to Nigeria’s National Malaria Elimination Programme (NMEP) planning, as it directly informs where intervention resources — ITN distribution, indoor residual spraying (IRS), seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) — should be prioritized. The 2023 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) provides nationally representative malaria testing data that allow this important epidemiologic question to be investigated.

Problem Statement

Although Nigeria continues to implement comprehensive malaria control programmes, disparities in malaria burden may persist between urban and rural populations. Identifying whether significant differences exist in malaria prevalence according to place of residence can help policymakers prioritize interventions, allocate resources efficiently, and strengthen malaria control strategies in high-risk communities.

2. Objective

To determine whether malaria test positivity among children aged 6–59 months differs significantly by residence type (urban vs. rural), using nationally representative Nigeria Malaria Indicator Survey (MIS) 2021 data.

3. Research Question

Is malaria test positivity among children aged 6–59 months significantly associated with place of residence (urban versus rural) in Nigeria?

4. Data Source

Survey: Nigeria Malaria Indicator Survey (MIS) 2021 — DHS Program

Datasets: NGKR81DT (Children’s Recode), NGHR81DT (Household Recode)

Design: Nationally representative, stratified, multi-stage cluster sample survey

Population: Children aged 6–59 months with a valid malaria test result (RDT and/or microscopy)

5. Study Design

Cross-sectional analysis of nationally representative survey data (Nigeria MIS 2021).

Population: Children aged 6–59 months residing in surveyed households across Nigeria’s 36 states +FCT.

Exposure: Type of residence: Urban, Rural

Outcome: Malaria test result (positive/negative) — RDT and/or microscopy confirmed

Categorical (binary)

6. Statistical Method

Chi-square test of independence (with a note on applying DHS sampling weights, given the complex survey design)

7. Interpretation

A Rao–Scott adjusted Chi-square test was conducted to examine the association between place of residence (urban/rural) and malaria prevalence among children aged 6-59 months in Nigeria using nationally representative, survey-weighted data from the 2021 Nigeria Malaria Indicator Survey (MIS) (DHS Program; n = 10,655). The weighted prevalence of malaria positivity was substantially higher among children residing in rural areas (26.7%, 95% CI [24.4%, 29.1%]) than among those living in urban areas (10.5%, 95% CI [8.6%, 12.8%]) (Rao–Scott adjusted F(1, 491) = 84.72, p < 0.001). The non-overplapping confidence intervals further confrim this difference is unlikely to be due to chance. This corresponds to a prevalence ratio of approximately 2.5, indicating that children living in rural areas were about 2.5 times as likely to test positive for malaria as those living in urban areas. These findings demonstrate a statistically significant and programmatically meaningful association between place of residence and malaria positivity in Nigeria, reinforcing the need for continued prioritization of rural-focused malaria control interventions (ITN -Insecticide Treated Nets -distributions, IRS -Indoor Residual Spraying, SMC- Seasonal Malaria Chemoprevention) within Nigeria’s National Malaria Elimination Programme.

8. Public Health Significance

The observed urban–rural disparity in malaria prevalence among children aged 6-59 months in Nigeria underscores the importance of place of residence as a key public health consideration in malaria control. The higher prevalence observed in rural areas suggests that malaria prevention programmes, including vector control, prompt diagnosis and treatment, seasonal malaria chemoprevention where appropriate, and community health education, should continue to prioritize rural populations. These findings provide evidence to inform policy decisions regarding the equitable distribution of malaria control resources and the strengthening of interventions in areas with the greatest disease burden.

Explanation on Weighted Sampling Estimates, Strata & Clusters

Sampling Weights

DHS surveys use a stratified, multi-stage cluster sampling design (hv005) — not a simple random sample of the whole country. In practice, this means certain states, urban/rural areas, or population subgroups may be deliberately oversampled or undersampled relative to their true share of the national population (i.e., different population subgroups are sampled at different rates). This is done intentionally, often to ensure smaller states or subgroups have enough sample size to produce statistically reliable prevalence estimates on their own — but it means the raw survey sample doesn’t proportionally represent Nigeria’s actual population composition.

Strata — ensuring representation across key subgroups

Before sampling begins, DHS divides the country into strata — typically each state crossed with urban/rural status (which is why the hv023 variable had 76 categories: roughly 37 states × urban/rural). Within each stratum, DHS deliberately draws its own independent sample. This guarantees that every state and every urban/rural combination is represented in the final data, rather than leaving it to chance (a pure random sample of Nigeria could, by bad luck, end up with very few respondents from a small state — stratification prevents that).

Clusters — accounting for the fact that neighbors are more alike than strangers

DHS doesn’t randomly select individual households scattered across the entire country — that would be logistically impossible. Instead, it randomly selects clusters (usually villages or enumeration areas — your hv021/cluster variable, with 567 of them in your data), then samples multiple households within each selected cluster.
